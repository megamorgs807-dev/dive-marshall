## Are there other BSAC branches or PADI centres active on this coast, and what do they say about the area?

### Takeaway
The nearest active, well-documented BSAC branch is the Ilfracombe and North Devon Sub-Aqua Club (BSAC86), which dives the Bristol Channel, Lundy, and the North Devon/Cornwall coast from a hard boat and RIBs, and which — unlike Bude's club — maintains a public list of named local wreck and drift-dive sites.

### Cited Findings
- Ilfracombe and North Devon Sub-Aqua Club dives "the Bristol Channel and Lundy weekly from their hard boat, Neptune, as well as organizing rib-away trips to Cornwall and beyond," regularly diving "the Bristol Channel, Lundy Island, the Cornish, South Devon and Dorset coasts and beyond," during a season of roughly April–October. — [Ilfracombe and North Devon Sub-Aqua Club](https://www.ilfracombediveclub.com/our-club/dive-sites/)
- Named site: "The George Lamb" — a broken wreck on Eastaway Rock on the North Devon coastline, a short boat trip from Ilfracombe Harbour, 12–20m depth, suitable for all grades. — [Ilfracombe and North Devon Sub-Aqua Club](https://www.ilfracombediveclub.com/our-club/dive-sites/)
- Named site: "The George Lockett" — a steamship lost 10 February 1982 east of Ilfracombe, 15–20m depth, suitable for Ocean Divers (BSAC's entry-level grade). — [Ilfracombe and North Devon Sub-Aqua Club](https://www.ilfracombediveclub.com/our-club/dive-sites/)
- Named drift dives: "Penns Port to Bennets Mouth" (frequent octopus, lobster, edible crab, fan worms, sponges) and "Lee Bay Drift Dive" (15–30m, suitable from Ocean Diver upward). — [Ilfracombe and North Devon Sub-Aqua Club](https://www.ilfracombediveclub.com/our-club/dive-sites/)
- Bude Sub Aqua Dive Club itself lists "Ilfracombe and North Devon Sub-Aqua Club" as its only named "Local BSAC Club" link, indicating the two clubs are aware of / affiliated with each other regionally. — [Bude Dive Club — Diving Organisation Links](https://www.budediveclub.co.uk/diving-organisation-links/)

[Note: this specific characterization comes from an AI-summarized fetch of cornwall-online.co.uk rather than a direct quote, so the "over three decades" and "only dedicated charter service" claims should be treated as moderately — not fully — verified.] — [Cornwall Online — Diving](https://www.cornwall-online.co.uk/waterside/diving.htm)

## What do diving magazines or guidebooks say about north Cornwall/north Devon diving specifically?

### Takeaway
Divernet (published by Scuba Diver Magazine) ran a dedicated January 2023 feature explicitly framing Cornwall's appeal around having two distinct coastlines close together, and names specific wrecks and reefs on both the north and south coasts, giving a grounded, named-site basis for comparison rather than generic tourism copy.

### Cited Findings
- Divernet/Scuba Diver Magazine, "Cornwall Is One Of The Most Popular Diving Spots In The UK" (by-lined to Scuba Diver Magazine, photos by Mark Evans, published 19 January 2023): "it boasts both a north-facing and south-facing coastline, separated in some cases by as little as a 20–30 minute drive." — [Divernet](https://divernet.com/scuba-diving/cornwall-is-one-of-the-most-popular-diving-spots-in-the-uk/)
- North coast wrecks named in that article: Princess Royal (2,000-tonne Scottish cargo vessel, torpedoed 1918, 40m), Syracusa (1,243-tonne schooner, sank 1897, 30m), Sphene (815-tonne steamship, sank 1946, 25m), and the St Chamond — nicknamed the "Train Wreck" because it "carries more steam locomotives on it than any other wreck, with at least six or seven locomotives" (28m). — [Divernet — Wreck Tour 38: The St Chamond](https://divernet.com/scuba-diving/wreck-tours/wreck-tour-38-the-st-chamond/); [Divernet](https://divernet.com/scuba-diving/cornwall-is-one-of-the-most-popular-diving-spots-in-the-uk/)
- North coast reef dives named: Poltexas Reef and Newquay's Headland (easy shore access, kelp beds, spider crabs, dogfish, pollock), and Porthminster Reef / "The Carracks" near St Ives (shallow, ~15m, rich fish/invertebrate life). — [Divernet](https://divernet.com/scuba-diving/cornwall-is-one-of-the-most-popular-diving-spots-in-the-uk/)
- South coast wrecks named for contrast: HMS Scylla (113m artificial reef, deliberately scuttled 2004, full penetration possible but silty interior), the James Eagan Layne (American Liberty ship, torpedoed 1945), HMS Elk (fishing vessel, sank 1940, 30–35m), the Carmarthen (4,262-tonne cargo steamship, torpedoed 1917, 20m), and SS Mohegan (passenger liner, sank 1898, 104 casualties). South coast reef sites named: Lamorna Cove, Silver Steps, Pendennis Point, the Welps, Eddystone, the Manacles. — [Divernet](https://divernet.com/scuba-diving/cornwall-is-one-of-the-most-popular-diving-spots-in-the-uk/)
- Divernet separately reports technical-diving discoveries off Cornwall at extreme depth, including British team "the Gasperados" finding the WWI-torpedoed US Coast Guard Cutter Tampa at 94m. ## What's the real contrast between this exposed north coast and Cornwall's south coast (Falmouth etc.) for diving — difficulty, crowds, character?

### Takeaway
Multiple independent sources converge on the same structural contrast: the north coast is more exposed to Atlantic swell, more weather-dependent, has fewer dive operators/less infrastructure, and rewards divers willing to work around conditions with dramatic topography and wreck density; the south coast (Falmouth, the Lizard, Scilly) is more sheltered, has a longer reliable season, and more developed charter/dive-centre infrastructure.

### Cited Findings
- "Cornwall is one of the most popular diving spots in the UK, mainly due to the fact that it boasts coastlines to the north and the south, so in inclement weather, you can usually still get a dive in somewhere" — the core structural reason the two coasts are discussed together at all. — [Divernet](https://divernet.com/scuba-diving/cornwall-is-one-of-the-most-popular-diving-spots-in-the-uk/)
- The north coast "is a wilder and more weather-dependent proposition," while "Falmouth Bay, the Lizard and the Isles of Scilly generally offer more sheltered, longer diving seasons." — [Cornwall Online — Diving](https://www.cornwall-online.co.uk/waterside/diving.htm) (also echoed in the Visit Cornwall TV north-coast guide summary)
- The south coast is explicitly described (relative to the north) as having "more-developed diving infrastructure," including well-known artificial-reef and Liberty-ship wrecks (HMS Scylla, James Eagan Layne) that are established, frequently-dived sites. — [Divernet](https://divernet.com/scuba-diving/cornwall-is-one-of-the-most-popular-diving-spots-in-the-uk/)
- The north coast, by contrast, "suits divers with a boat operator who knows the local conditions, rather than casual shore diving," with Newquay as the main charter base and Padstow's sheltered Camel Estuary as the fallback when swell closes the exposed headlands — i.e., the north coast's charter diving is itself fragile/weather-contingent even where it exists. — [Visit Cornwall TV / Cornwall Online, cross-corroborated]
- Bude Sub Aqua Dive Club's own FAQ is a concrete, first-party data point supporting this contrast from the "demand side": the club chooses to take routine day trips to South Devon/Dorset rather than diving its own doorstep north Cornwall coast, reserving Cornwall itself for longer, less frequent weekend trips. — [Bude Dive Club FAQ](https://www.budediveclub.co.uk/faq/)
- On "crowds": no source directly quantified or compared visitor/diver density between the two coasts. The closest indirect evidence is tourism-description of Millook itself as having "difficult access and no close facilities" and being "rarely busy" — consistent with a generally low-traffic north-coast character near Bude, but this is about the beach/cove generally, not diving traffic specifically. — [North Cornwall Rocks — Millook](https://www.northcornwallrocks.co.uk/things-to-do/millook)

### Inferences
- Taken together, the honest "character" distinction is: north coast = wilder, more wreck-dense on paper, less reliably diveable, less crowded/less commercially developed; south coast = calmer, better-established flagship wrecks and dive centres, longer dependable season, presumably busier as a result of that reliability and infrastructure. This is a defensible, source-backed contrast rather than invented hype — but the "less crowded" half is inferred from general coastal-access description, not from any source that measured or compared diver numbers directly.

### Gaps
- No source provided a direct, quantified or even qualitative comparison of diver crowding/congestion between the two coasts (e.g., number of dive boats, how busy named sites get). This would need targeted operator-review or forum research (e.g., TripAdvisor/Google reviews of specific charters) beyond this research pass.
- No source specifically contrasted Bude's immediate coastline (as opposed to "the north coast" broadly, i.e., Newquay/Padstow/St Ives) against the south coast. The contrast evidenced here is coast-vs-coast (north Cornwall generally vs south Cornwall), not Bude-vs-Falmouth specifically.
