# Marine Life, Kelp Forests & Underwater Habitat: Hartland Point to Crackington Haven, and Lundy Island

Scope note: Primary, location-confirmed documentation for this exact stretch comes almost entirely from two sources: (1) Lundy Island's own marine conservation programme (Landmark Trust/Lundy Company, Lundy Field Society, the Lundy MCZ Advisory Group, and the Hoskin et al. 2009 No-Take Zone study), which is the best-documented site by far, and (2) the Defra/JNCC "Hartland Point to Tintagel" Marine Conservation Zone designation, which covers the mainland coast including Bude, Widemouth and Crackington Haven. Diver-specific (as opposed to angler-specific or tourism-site) reporting for the mainland stretch between Hartland Point and Crackington Haven proved sparse in available sources — this is flagged explicitly below wherever it applies.

## What fish species are specifically reported by divers in this area?

### Takeaway
Lundy has solid primary documentation of pollack and two wrasse species (ballan and cuckoo), plus bull huss, red band fish, tope and conger eel as species anglers/divers are asked to protect and report. For the mainland Hartland–Bude–Crackington Haven stretch, available sources are angling reports rather than diver reports, so species presence (wrasse, pollack, conger, dogfish, huss) is well evidenced for the water column but not confirmed as diver sightings specifically.

### Cited Findings
- Lundy's reef/kelp fauna documented by the official ecological baseline for the island includes pollack (*Pollachius pollachius*) and wrasses, specifically ballan wrasse (*Labrus bergylta*) and cuckoo wrasse (*Labrus bimaculatus*), alongside "seasonal visitors such as the sun fish (*Mola mola*) and basking shark (*Cetorhinus maximus*)" — [Hoskin, Coleman & von Carlshausen, "Ecological effects of the Lundy NTZ: 2003 to 2007"](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- Lundy's Marine Protected Area Code of Conduct instructs sea anglers to "release all wrasse species, tope, red band fish, bull huss and conger eels where they are caught" — implying these are the species regularly caught/encountered around the island — [Diving Lundy Information Sheet, compiled by Joe Parker, Lundy Warden, rev. Feb 2024](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/activities-and-things-to-do/diving/diving-lundy-information-sheet_2024.pdf)
- Bull huss (*Scyliorhinus stellatus*) egg cases have shown increased abundance at Lundy over the past five years, found entangled around branching sponges — [Lundy Marine Protected Area – Important Marine Communities](https://www.lundympa.org.uk/marine-life/important-marine-communities)
- Anglers fishing from Lundy's shore and boats "mainly catch species such as mackerel, pollack, bass, dogfish and rays," while scuba divers on Lundy "mainly target shellfish such as scallops, crabs and lobsters, but may also take flatfish (e.g. plaice) and rays opportunistically" (attributed to Chris Davis, Natural England, and Dr Keith Hiscock, Marine Biological Association of the UK) — [Hoskin et al. 2009, Lundy NTZ report](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- For the mainland coast: angling reports describe Hartland Point as "a classic rough-ground mark" good for wrasse and pollack by lure/float fishing in settled seas, with night sessions producing bull huss, conger and pouting; fishing west of Hartland Point has yielded pollack, dogfish, pouting, black bream, huss, smoothound, tope and ballan wrasse — [North Devon Angling News](https://www.northdevonanglingnews.co.uk/tag/clovelly/); [Where's The Fish, Hartland Quay](https://wheresthefish.uk/south-west/devon/town/hartland-quay)
- Milook, near Bude, is reported as good for huss and conger at night with occasional bass, and nearby marks reported for decent wrasse — [Where's The Fish](https://wheresthefish.uk/south-west/devon/town/hartland-quay) (aggregated angling-mark information)

### Inferences
- The species list for Lundy (pollack, ballan wrasse, cuckoo wrasse, bull huss, conger, tope, red band fish) is drawn from an official ecological/management source and can be treated as genuinely diver/site-specific to Lundy.
- The mainland fish species list (wrasse, pollack, conger, bull huss, pouting, dogfish, black bream, smoothound, tope, bass) is evidenced by shore and boat angling reports for marks between Bude and Hartland Point, which is a reasonable proxy for what divers would also encounter on the same reef ground, but it is NOT a diver-sourced or survey-sourced record — it should be flagged as inferred/general rather than confirmed diver reporting.

### Gaps
- No diver trip report, BSAC newsletter, or Seasearch record naming specific fish sightings between Hartland Point and Crackington Haven (mainland) was found in available sources. The one wreck-diving account found with named fish species (pollack, cuckoo wrasse, on the wreck *Poldown*) is located off Trevose Head — well south of this assignment's stretch — and is explicitly NOT included as evidence for this coast, only noted as nearby North Cornwall context: [Divernet, Wreck Tour 164: The Poldown](https://divernet.com/scuba-diving/wreck-tours/wreck-tour-164-the-poldown/).

## What crustaceans/shellfish are reported (crayfish/spiny lobster, crabs, lobster)?

### Takeaway
Lundy has by far the richest and most quantitative crustacean data of anywhere on this coast, driven by a four-year scientific study of its No-Take Zone (NTZ): lobster, brown/edible crab, velvet swimming crab and spider crab were all specifically monitored, with lobster showing the strongest documented recovery. Spiny lobster/crawfish is specifically protected and monitored at Lundy via a dedicated Seasearch recording scheme. No equivalent crustacean data was found for the mainland Hartland–Crackington Haven stretch.

### Cited Findings
- The Lundy NTZ monitoring programme (2003–2007) tracked four commercially-fished crustacean species: lobster (*Homarus gammarus*), edible/brown crab (*Cancer pagurus*), velvet swimming crab (*Necora puber*) and spider crab (*Maja squinado*), plus scallop (*Pecten maximus*) — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- By 2007, landable-sized lobsters (carapace length ≥90mm) were 427% more abundant inside the Lundy NTZ than in control/reference fishing grounds; the mean abundance of landable lobsters was already 205% greater than control areas when monitoring began in 2004 (18 months post-designation), then rose a further 127% by 2007. Landable lobsters also showed a small but statistically significant ~5% increase in average size relative to fished areas — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- Undersized lobsters (carapace length <90mm) increased 97% in the NTZ and 124% in adjacent control areas from 2004–2007, interpreted (cautiously, not proven) as possible "spillover" of juveniles from the protected zone into fished grounds — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- Brown crab showed no change in relative abundance in the NTZ but did show a significant 25% increase in size relative to control/reference areas — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- Velvet crab abundance declined 65% (NTZ) and 75% (adjacent control) but rose 26% in more distant reference sites; researchers suggest this may be due to increased predation/competition from the larger lobster population inside the NTZ — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- Spider crab and scallop populations showed no detectable effect of the NTZ; scallop populations at both NTZ and control sites were described as being at "very low density," dominated by old individuals with no significant recent recruitment — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- A December 2013 byelaw banned taking crawfish (spiny lobster, *Palinurus elephas*) from Lundy's Marine Conservation Zone entirely; Seasearch runs a dedicated Crawfish recording scheme for snorkellers and divers at Lundy (the UK's first MCZ), with records submitted via iRecord — [Diving Lundy Information Sheet](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/activities-and-things-to-do/diving/diving-lundy-information-sheet_2024.pdf)
- Diver-caught shellfish (scallops, crabs, lobster) around Lundy legally requires a Devon and Severn IFCA licence, though many local dive charters operate a no-take policy and discourage capture — [Diving Lundy Information Sheet](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/activities-and-things-to-do/diving/diving-lundy-information-sheet_2024.pdf)
- Lundy's main commercial fishery, historically and at the time of the NTZ study, was potting for lobster and crab, plus diver-harvesting of lobster, crab and scallops — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)

### Inferences
- The scale of the lobster recovery data (a >4x abundance increase within under four years inside a protected zone smaller than 5km²) is strong, specific evidence that Lundy's waters are genuinely crustacean-rich relative to fished Devon/Cornwall grounds, not just a generic "UK has lobsters" claim.
- The absence of comparable crustacean survey data for the mainland Hartland–Crackington Haven stretch suggests this length of coast has not had an equivalent structured study; general UK/Cornwall knowledge (brown crab, lobster, spider crab present on rocky reef) likely applies but is not specifically confirmed here.

### Gaps
- No diver-specific crustacean sighting reports (e.g. crayfish/spiny lobster sightings, named dive sites with crab/lobster abundance) were found for the mainland coast between Hartland Point and Crackington Haven.

## Are grey seals specifically reported interacting with divers on this coast? Where?

### Takeaway
Grey seal–diver interaction is extremely well documented at Lundy specifically — it's one of the island's signature diving draws, with ~200 resident seals and named haul-out sites. No equivalent documented diver–seal interaction was found for the mainland Hartland Point-to-Crackington Haven coast in the sources checked; Bude's own tourism material only mentions seals in passing as something to "keep an eye out for."

### Cited Findings
- "Lundy has a breeding colony of approximately 200 Atlantic grey seals. These beautiful animals enjoy interacting with divers, snorkelers and swimmers, and will often initiate play... Seals are naturally inquisitive, however, they are also cautious." — [Diving Lundy Information Sheet, Joe Parker, Lundy Warden](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/activities-and-things-to-do/diving/diving-lundy-information-sheet_2024.pdf)
- Lundy's code of conduct names specific seal haul-out locations where boats/divers must keep 100m distance year-round: Brazen Ward, Three-quarter Wall Bay and Surf Point — [Diving Lundy Information Sheet](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/activities-and-things-to-do/diving/diving-lundy-information-sheet_2024.pdf)
- The Lundy No-Take Zone report states Lundy "provides rich feeding grounds and an important breeding site for grey seal (*Halichoerus grypus*)" as part of the island's designated ecological importance — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- Grey seals are described as Lundy's "best known resident," supporting "the largest breeding colony in south-west England" — [search summary citing Landmark Trust/Lundy sources](https://meanderingwild.com/lundy-island-seals/); [iKelite blog, "Lundy Island: The Best Seal Diving in the World"](https://www.ikelite.com/blogs/features/lundy-island-the-best-seal-diving-in-the-world)
- Lundy's official code of conduct explicitly instructs divers and snorkellers: "Whilst snorkeling or diving, please refrain from touching or feeding the seals... Do not directly approach the seals on land or in the water or attempt to chase them, given time they will come to you" — confirming active, routine, close-range diver-seal encounters are the norm and are formally managed — [Diving Lundy Information Sheet](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/activities-and-things-to-do/diving/diving-lundy-information-sheet_2024.pdf)
- A Bude tourism page states visitors can "keep an eye out for seals and the occasional dolphin and basking shark in the waters off Bude's coastline" — [Visit Bude, Wildlife in Bude](https://www.visitbude.info/wildlife-in-bude/) — this is a general tourism statement, not a diver or survey report, and is flagged as lower-confidence/non-specific.

### Inferences
- Diver-seal interaction as a defined, named attraction (with charter operators branding trips as "Seal Encounter Dive Trips") is a Lundy-specific phenomenon in this assignment's scope, not something documented for the mainland coast.

### Gaps
- No source was found reporting divers specifically encountering/interacting with grey seals at Hartland Point or anywhere else on the mainland coast between Hartland Point and Crackington Haven. Seals are known to be present generally in this part of the Bristol Channel/North Devon coast, but a specific diver-interaction report for the mainland was not located.

## What is the kelp forest like here specifically?

### Takeaway
Lundy's kelp forest is scientifically documented and unusual: a mix of the typical northern canopy species alongside an expanding population of the warm-water "golden kelp" (*Laminaria ochroleuca*) that is at its northern range limit there, with specific shifts tracked over decades. No equivalent diver- or survey-documented kelp description was found for the mainland Hartland–Crackington Haven stretch.

### Cited Findings
- The kelp forest at Lundy is dominated by oarweed (*Laminaria digitata*), with sea belt (*Saccharina latissima*) and furbellows (*Saccorhiza polyschides*) also present; north of Rat Island, a high proportion of plants are golden kelp, a warm-water species at its northern limit of distribution at Lundy — [The Lundy Field Society – Other Marine Life](http://lundy.org.uk/about-lundy/wildlife-in-the-sea/other-marine-life) (note: sourced from a search-engine summary of this page rather than a direct fetch, so treat species list as moderate rather than fully verified confidence)
- A long-term survey (guided originally by Dr Keith Hiscock in the 1970s, building on intensive subtidal investigation by the Lundy Field Society) found that the proportion of southern kelp (*Laminaria ochroleuca*) relative to northern kelp — cuvie, (*Laminaria hyperborea*) — increased dramatically at two shallow water sites on Lundy's east coast: Gannets' Bay and north of Rat Island — [Lundy Field Society Annual Report, cited via Lundy Marine Management Plan 2017](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy_marine_management_plan_2017.pdf)
- Despite the increased proportion of golden kelp, overall kelp forest communities at Lundy were assessed as being "in overall favourable condition" — [Lundy Marine Management Plan 2017 / LFS reporting, per search summary](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy_marine_management_plan_2017.pdf)
- "Kelp forest communities" are formally listed as a legally protected feature of Lundy's Special Area of Conservation (SAC) designation — [Lundy Marine Protected Area – Important Marine Communities](https://www.lundympa.org.uk/marine-life/important-marine-communities)
- Independent peer-reviewed research confirms *Laminaria ochroleuca* (golden kelp) has "extended its distribution from sheltered coasts and has progressed eastwards and northwards onto Lundy Island in the Bristol Channel," with surveys indicating temporally stable populations at its northernmost sites on Lundy; its northern range limit broadly corresponds to the 10°C sea-temperature isotherm — [Smale et al., "The rise of Laminaria ochroleuca in the Western English Channel," via ResearchGate/Wernberg Lab](https://wernberglab.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/03/2015_smale-et-al-ma-ecol-lam-ochroleuca.pdf)
- The adjacent mainland MCZ (Hartland Point to Tintagel, covering this entire assigned stretch) formally protects "moderate energy infralittoral rock" and "high energy infralittoral rock" — the shallow, wave-exposed rocky reef habitat type that typically supports kelp forest — as well as "moderate/high energy circalittoral rock" in deeper water dominated by sponges, anemones and sea-fan corals rather than kelp — [Defra, Hartland Point to Tintagel MCZ Factsheet, 17 January 2016](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a80d86e40f0b62305b8d796/mcz-hartland-point-tintagel-factsheet.pdf)

### Inferences
- Lundy's kelp forest is notably unusual among UK sites for hosting a documented, climate-linked shift from the normal UK canopy species (*Laminaria hyperborea*) toward a warm-water congener (*L. ochroleuca*) at specific named locations (Gannets' Bay, north of Rat Island) — this is a genuinely site-specific, non-generic finding.
- The Hartland Point to Tintagel MCZ designation implies kelp-supporting infralittoral rock habitat is present and protected along the mainland stretch (Bude, Widemouth, Crackington Haven included), but this is an inference from habitat-type protection, not a direct diver or survey description of the kelp itself.

### Gaps
- No source specified which kelp species dominate the mainland reefs between Hartland Point and Crackington Haven, nor gave depth ranges or density for kelp forest on this specific stretch. By general UK west-coast norms *Laminaria hyperborea* (cuvie) would be the expected dominant canopy species on exposed rock in the 0–10m zone, but this is a general inference, not a confirmed site-specific fact, and should be presented to the report writer as such.

## Any notable or unusual marine life sightings specific to this coast?

### Takeaway
Lundy has multiple well-documented unusual/notable species tied specifically to its location: five coral species at or near their range limits (several now in documented decline since the 1970s–80s), the red band fish, and seasonal sunfish/basking sharks. For the mainland stretch, the one strong, location-specific finding is the formal MCZ protection of pink sea-fan coral and honeycomb worm reefs between Hartland Point and Tintagel; basking shark/dolphin sightings off Bude itself are only weakly sourced (tourism copy, not survey data).

### Cited Findings
- Lundy's position "on the edge of the Atlantic" and in a warm water current is reported (via a dive-tourism aggregator, not an official source) to bring "rare, warm water species to the island including five different types of corals" — [I Dream of Mangoes, "Lundy Island Diving" guide](https://www.idreamofmangoes.com/post/lundy-island-scuba-diving-with-seals/) (flagged: tourism/blog source, moderate confidence)
- Several coral/anthozoan species at Lundy have declined in abundance since the 1970s–1980s according to the island's official marine communities page: sunset cup-coral (*Leptopsammia pruvoti*), pink sea fan (*Eunicella verrucosa*), southern cup coral (*Caryophyllia inornata*), Weymouth carpet coral (*Hoplangia durotrix*) and red sea fingers (*Alcyonium glomeratum*); plumose anemones are noted growing on Lundy's shipwrecks — [Lundy Marine Protected Area – Important Marine Communities](https://www.lundympa.org.uk/marine-life/important-marine-communities)
- Lundy's marine life recording effort specifically calls out interest in "non-native seaweeds and gelatinous jellyfish... colorful corals, crawfish, red band fish and silly seals" as notable/target species for diver sightings — [Diving Lundy Information Sheet](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/activities-and-things-to-do/diving/diving-lundy-information-sheet_2024.pdf)
- Lundy's code of conduct requires a 5-knot speed reduction within 100m of basking sharks and cetaceans (whales, dolphins, porpoises) as well as seals — implying these are expected, regularly-encountered animals in Lundy's waters, not rare curiosities — [Diving Lundy Information Sheet](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/activities-and-things-to-do/diving/diving-lundy-information-sheet_2024.pdf)
- Sunfish (*Mola mola*) and basking shark (*Cetorhinus maximus*) are documented as "seasonal visitors" to Lundy's waters in the island's own ecological baseline literature — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- The Hartland Point to Tintagel MCZ (covering the whole mainland stretch of this assignment, from Hartland Point to Tintagel Head, following the coastline past Bude, Widemouth and Crackington Haven) specifically designates and protects: pink sea-fan coral (*Eunicella verrucosa*) colonies on circalittoral rock, and honeycomb worm (*Sabellaria alveolata*) reefs — intertidal reef structures built from colonial worm sand-tubes that can extend "tens of metres across and up to a metre tall," supporting anemones, snails, shore crabs and seaweeds — [Defra, Hartland Point to Tintagel MCZ Factsheet, 17 January 2016](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a80d86e40f0b62305b8d796/mcz-hartland-point-tintagel-factsheet.pdf)
- "Fragile sponge & anthozoan communities" on subtidal rocky habitat are also a protected feature of this same mainland MCZ, described as a mosaic of sponges, anemones and sea-fan corals on deeper circalittoral rock — [Defra MCZ Factsheet](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a80d86e40f0b62305b8d796/mcz-hartland-point-tintagel-factsheet.pdf)
- A Bude tourism page mentions seals, "the occasional dolphin and basking shark in the waters off Bude's coastline" — [Visit Bude, Wildlife in Bude](https://www.visitbude.info/wildlife-in-bude/) (flagged: general tourism copy, not a survey/diver source)
- Cornwall-wide: basking sharks typically appear from mid/late May as the spring plankton bloom develops and depart in autumn; this is general Cornwall seasonal biology, not specific to this stretch — [Wildlife Insight, Seawatching in Cornwall](https://www.wildlifeinsight.com/seawatching-in-cornwall/) (flagged as general, not site-specific)

### Inferences
- The honeycomb worm reef and pink sea-fan coral designations are a genuinely distinctive, location-specific finding for this exact mainland stretch (Hartland to Tintagel, i.e. including Bude/Widemouth/Crackington Haven) and are a strong candidate for the report's mainland-coast section, since they come from a formal government designation document naming this precise coastline.
- Where Lundy is concerned, there is a credible pattern across multiple sources (official and informal) of warm-water/range-edge species (golden kelp, several coral species, the red band fish) being notable precisely because Lundy sits at a climatic and oceanographic crossover point in the Bristol Channel — this is consistent across sources and not just a single blog's claim.

This should be flagged to the report writer as a named entity worth a follow-up targeted search if time allows, rather than something confirmed here.

## Lundy as a Marine Conservation Zone/no-take zone — what does this mean for marine life richness, and what's documented?

### Takeaway
Lundy's no-take zone (NTZ) is one of the UK's best-studied small marine reserves, with a rigorous four-year before/after, in/out comparison (Hoskin et al. 2009) that found clear, large, specific increases in lobster abundance and size, plus knock-on effects on crab populations — concrete, quantified evidence of conservation-driven marine life richness, not a generic claim.

### Cited Findings
- Lundy was established as a voluntary Marine Nature Reserve in 1971, formalised as the UK's first statutory Marine Nature Reserve in 1986, designated a Special Area of Conservation in 2000, and became the UK's first Marine Conservation Zone in 2010; a 4km²-scale No-Take Zone to the east of the island has prohibited all fishing and collection of sea life since 2003 — [Diving Lundy Information Sheet](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/activities-and-things-to-do/diving/diving-lundy-information-sheet_2024.pdf); [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- After four years of monitoring (to 2007), only three of the 20 species monitored across the whole study showed a measurable NTZ effect — all three were commercially-fished crustaceans: lobster, brown crab and velvet crab. There was no detectable NTZ effect on spider crab, scallops, or the sessile sponge/anemone/coral assemblage on circalittoral rock — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- "Lobster provided the most compelling evidence for a positive effect of the NTZ, both in terms of the multiplicity of evidence and the magnitude of changes" — landable lobsters were 427% more abundant in the NTZ than control/reference fished areas by 2007 — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- The study's authors explicitly caution that effects on undersized lobster ("spillover" into surrounding fished grounds) have "not been proved" and that "there is no obvious and straightforward mechanism" confirmed for it — a conflict/caveat the report writer should preserve rather than oversell — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- The study's authors recommend continued monitoring and streamer-tagging work, noting effects on lobster and crab "are [not] fully developed" as of 2007 — i.e., the documented richness may still be increasing, not a one-off plateau — [Hoskin et al. 2009](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/conservation/lundy-ntz.pdf)
- Separately from the NTZ, a December 2013 byelaw bans the taking of crawfish (spiny lobster) from Lundy's entire Marine Conservation Zone (not just the NTZ) — a broader, species-specific protection measure — [Diving Lundy Information Sheet](https://www.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/1-aa-new-responsive-site-images/website/lundy/discover-lundy/activities-and-things-to-do/diving/diving-lundy-information-sheet_2024.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Lundy NTZ data is a rare case in UK marine conservation of a well-controlled, multi-year, published scientific study directly tying a no-take designation to measurable increases in marine life abundance/size — this gives the report writer genuinely strong, specific, "not generic" material for this key question.
- The fact that only crustaceans (not the reef's fish, scallops, or sessile invertebrates) showed a detectable effect after four years is itself informative and should be reported honestly rather than implying the NTZ transformed the whole ecosystem.

### Gaps
- No more recent (post-2009) published follow-up study updating these NTZ trends was found in available search results; the Lundy Marine Management Plan 2017 and LFS Annual Report (2016/19) references found via search touch on kelp and coral trends but a full quantitative crustacean update beyond 2007 was not located in the time available.
